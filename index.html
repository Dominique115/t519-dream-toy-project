the client

Meet Evan

Before any cutting or gluing, we sat down with Evan to interview him about his dream toy — his favorite things to play, who he plays with, and what he's into. He was quite specific on his idea of a rocket, he wanted a small rocket that could glow in the dark. We also learnt that he likes to play boardgames and cooperatively, so we both had the idea of building a board game.

We came out the interview with a clear goal: A rocket, built for a kid who loves space — with room to grow into something he could play with in more than one way.

the idea

Teaming up

We paired up with Riya and Clemence to talk through our ideas. They both pointed out that a board game with a strict rule set could be limiting — it might box in Evan's imagination instead of opening it up.

So we decided to keep it simple: give Evan a few example rules, but leave the rest open for him to invent his own way to play.

the stars

Where the idea came from

While messing around in the lab, I was fiddling with a box of loose buttons when a handful spilled onto a fabric. Scattered like that, I think they looked like stars in the night sky.

That was it — a game where you toss "stars" onto the board and try to find constellations among them.

I liked the look of buttons for stars, but they kept bouncing all over the place when I dropped them onto the board… what should I try?

↺ tried this first I glued small magnets onto the backs of the buttons. They stuck to the board nicely — but then I realized the buttons all stick to each other when stored together in a small box. Not quite it.

✓ this is what actually worked I tried laser-cutting the stars out of acrylic instead of using buttons. They're lighter, don't roll away, and don't clump together in storage — exactly what the game needed.

the cards

Making the game cards

With the stars sorted, we designed a full celestial deck in Figma — 54 constellations plus quite a few blank cards, each with a point value on the upper left corner, based on how many stars it takes to trace.

Each card shows the constellation's shape on the front, with its name and a short story on the back. We also designed a second, smaller deck of action cards — things like Stellar Wind, Black Hole, and Warp Jump — that let players trade stars, swap cards, or shake up the board mid-game.

Once the cards existed, we tested whether they actually worked with the stars on the board; tracing a constellation card against the scattered pieces to see if the shape held up.

the rocket

Making it glow

We wanted the rocket to glow in the dark. I tested a few painted prototypes, comparing paint markers, acrylic, against layers of actual glow-in-the-dark paint.

Haytch pointed out that glow paint needs to absorb light first, and usually needs several layers before it actually glows as expected. Lesson learned — thanks, Haytch! Under UV light, it finally showed...a little...

the rocket

Lighting it up for real

Glow paint was nice, but what if the rocket actually lit up when it landed on the metal board? We planned to wire an LED into the rocket, connected through magnets touching the board. But soon we discovered it is not feasible.

After a quick Electrics 101 lesson with Haytch: electrons need a complete, defined path to flow along. The metal board has no positive or negative terminals — so there was no path for the electrons to follow.

The metal board has no positive or negative terminals, so the electrons have nowhere to go… what's missing?

↺ not the route we took A battery would power the LED, but it doesn't solve the real problem — the board itself still has no defined positive/negative path for current to travel through.

✓ this is what actually worked I made a parallel circuit with dedicated nodes for the rocket to complete, using copper tape as the conductive path on the fabric — one strip per leg of the LED, connected back to a battery pack.

the rocket

Getting the LED inside

Easier said than done. The rocket body is 3D printed — so how do you get a fragile LED and its wires inside a closed shape?

How do I get the LED into the rocket body?

↺ tried this first In the first prototype, I tried inserting the LED and wire through the window, then pulling the wire out from the base. The window turned out too small for this plan.

↺ tried this second Next, I split the bottom part, inserted the LED from the base upward, then glued the two halves together. It worked — but I didn't like the look of the visible seam.

✓ this is what actually worked In the third attempt, we paused the 3D print midway, inserted the LED and wire by hand, then let the printer continue on top of it — so the rocket comes out as one seamless piece.

Marc reminded us to add insulation between the LED legs, since they sat very close together with a real risk of short-circuiting. We also learned to use tree supports and a custom G-code pause so Prusa would stop automatically right when we needed to drop the LED in.

the base board

Wiring the board underneath

The rocket's LED is only half the circuit. The other half lives in the base: three laser-cut boards, one coin battery, a switch, two magnets, and a lot of conductive tape. The circuit only closes once the boards are stacked together.

Here's how the layers fit. Drag the model to look around, peek underneath to see the tape on the back of each board, then snap the layers together.

drag to rotate · arrow keys work too

  • Board 1 · score boardFront: the scoring track, two magnet holes, and the switch. Back: tape behind each magnet, plus a strip from the + magnet through the switch to where the battery's + side lands.
  • Board 2 · battery layerFront: the coin battery (+ side up), a pocket for the bumps on the back of the switch, and the − tape poking up through a small hole. Back: tape from the battery's − side over to that hole.
  • Board 3 · coverA blank board that closes everything in and holds the battery in place.

On the rocket's side, we soldered a magnet onto each LED wire at the bottom of the rocket, so its two feet click onto the board's magnets. This is the same soldering step that weakened the magnets (more on that next).

Once the tape paths tested well, we glued the boards together so the layers stay lined up and the circuit stays closed. The stacked boards became the lid of the box.

And then it worked. The rocket landed on the board, the circuit closed, and the LED lit up.

the rocket

A magnet mystery

Right after soldering the magnets onto the rocket's LED wires, they felt noticeably weaker than before.

After soldering, the magnets felt weaker… what should I try?

✓ this is what we actually did Turns out magnets lose magnetism when exposed to high heat — exactly what soldering does. I rubbed the weakened magnets against larger, stronger ones to restore some of their pull. It worked… a little.

↺ not the route we took New magnets would sidestep the problem; but the soldering issue is still there...

the planets

Building out the solar system

Liam found cute planet patterns online, and we cut them out of felt and fabric, adding magnets inside so they can interact with the metal board.

A bit of scrap felt glued within the planet layers made each planet feel soft, and little details gave each one its own character.

For the board itself, we found an old dark blue t-shirt and sewed it into a case — which meant relearning how to use a sewing machine (Thanks Marc!)

the board

Building the score track

Alongside the space board, we laser-cut a separate scoring track — a winding path from start to 50 points, with little rocket icons marking the way. We embedded the battery circuit inside the scoring board so it lights the rocket up at the winning 50 block; it's also the lid for our storage box of all the game components.

putting it together

Two ways to play

  1. The rocket can land on different planets and roam around the space board.
  2. Clear all the planets, and the board becomes an open area for Evan's own board game. We designed multiple rules for him, and made a rule book that fits into the box.

We sewed the rule book with sewing machine, binding the pages together, and left blank pages at the back for Evan to write his own rules. (Thanks Marc for the sewing machine support!)

Everything packs away into a laser-cut box with its own compartments.

Along the way, Mimi suggested different-sized stars could carry different point values, and Clemence's early feedback was that the design felt genuinely new. We incorporated Mimi's rule idea into the rulebook, and Clemence's feedback gave us confidence in the game. We wanted the game to have a high ceiling and wide walls, so Evan can keep coming up with his own ways to play.

a side story

Then we saw Orion

Last Thursday night, walking out of HGSE, we looked up and spotted Orion: the three stars of his belt in a neat row, with his shoulders and feet around them.

After weeks of tracing constellations on acrylic stars and cards, finding one in the real sky felt kind of amazing. It's the same thing we hope Evan does with the game and actual night skies: look at a scatter of stars and find a shape in it.
